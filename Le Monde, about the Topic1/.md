Switzerland drags its feet in tracking down Russian oligarchs' assets
Two months after announcing the implementation of European sanctions, Bern is suspected of ill will. Asset seizures are still very modest.
By Serge Enderlin (Geneva (France) correspondent)

Published on May 11, 2022, at 5:48 pm (Paris), updated on May 11, 2022, at 5:48 pm

The numbers are mind-boggling. The total of the Russian oligarchs' hoard sheltered in Switzerland varies between 150 and 200 billion Swiss francs (143 and 190 billion euros) in combined deposits and assets, according to the Swiss Bankers Association. "If the Swiss bankers admit it, we can easily double the amount," said Anglo-American banker Bill Browder, who has been tracking the financial turpitudes of the Kremlin and its affiliates for a decade, after being defrauded in Russia. His Russian lawyer, Sergei Magnitsky, died in prison in Moscow. He appeared last week before a US congressional committee seeking to shed light on Switzerland's role, "long known as a destination for war criminals and kleptocrats to hide their loot, and a key ally of Russian dictator Vladimir Putin and his cronies."

The exchanges soon turned into an indictment of Bern's alleged unwillingness to implement sanctions against Russia. Only 7.5 billion Swiss francs (7.15 billion euros) have been frozen so far. "There is something seriously rotten in Switzerland," said Mr. Browder. In Bern, the Federal Council (government) immediately denounced "politically unacceptable allegations," while the acting president, Foreign Minister Ignazio Cassis, informed the U.S. State Department of his "indignation." According to government spokesman André Simonazzi, Switzerland applies international standards against money laundering and tax evasion.

'Deck of cards of oligarchs in Switzerland'
The banishment of Russia by the international community has provoked a brutal realignment of power balances, including financial ones, for which Switzerland was not prepared. In recent days, calls have begun to be made for the executive to step into a higher gear and recognize the importance of the moment.

"Our authorities are acting as if they wanted to preserve the relationship between the Swiss financial center and large Russian fortunes, because one day the war will be over, and it would be a shame to deprive ourselves of this opportunity that has been so profitable. The Ministry of Economic Affairs, for example, has not set up a special structure to identify Russian funds, and is not participating in the efforts of Washington and the EU," said Nicolas Walder, a Green MP from Geneva and member of the Parliament's foreign affairs committee.

The NGO Public Eye came up with a booster jab: On Monday, May 9, it sent its "deck of cards of oligarchs in Switzerland" to the 200 Swiss MPs by email. It consisted of 32 cards bearing the portraits of billionaires closely linked to the Kremlin and well established in the Alpine country. Between them, they are worth 293 billion Swiss francs. Each card shows an individual, the amount of his fortune and the length of his yacht. The tease is facetious, but the matter is very serious.

“It's a way of reminding elected officials and the government of their responsibility," said Géraldine Viret of Public Eye. "Contrary to general belief, the Swiss like to take risks if there is money at stake, and with Russia, there was no lack of money. On the other hand, they don't like to admit having made a mistake and backing down. In a context as dramatic as war, financial opacity is no longer acceptable. The country is once again entering a turbulent zone, with maximum risk to its reputation."

Loopholes in the money laundering legislation
Despite the gradual lifting of banking secrecy under joint pressure from the United States and the European Union, the Swiss Confederation has remained a unique haven to which the world's largest fortunes continue to flock in order to escape the curiosity of the tax authorities and public opinion. When the storm grows, a defense argument is often used in Geneva and Zurich: The criticism is said to come primarily from competing financial centers, beginning with London; not to mention the American state of Delaware, of which President Joe Biden is the most eminent citizen. Faced with these attacks, the Swiss authorities plead the probity of their financial sector.

Commodity trading, foundations, real estate, lawyers – sectors where the Anti Money Laundering Act (AMLA) does not apply are however still widespread in Switzerland, like so many loopholes into which Russian billionaires and their highly paid intermediaries have rushed. Take Sulayman Kerimov, an oligarch from Dagestan who has always kept in good standing with the Kremlin. Public Eye's 6C card says his fortune is 14.69 billion Swiss francs and that he has two properties in Switzerland. His yacht, Ice, spans 90 meters. And the foundation in the canton of Lucerne managing his fortune is under the name of an illustrious stranger, a local tattoo artist who doesn't speak a word of Russian
