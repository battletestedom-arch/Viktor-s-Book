Oil traders' windfall profits highlight paradoxes of Swiss model
Geneva is full of rumors about the record profits made by oil market players. The legal framework in which they operate has drawn criticism and sparked calls for tighter regulation.
By Serge Enderlin (Geneva (Switzerland) correspondent)

SÉVERIN MILLET
In Geneva, the millions of dollars gained by oil traders are not going unnoticed. SuisseNégoce, the lobby representing commodity traders, has expressed concern about several recent violent home-jackings (burglaries committed while residents are present) that it says authorities failed to prevent. The organization called on the canton to urgently deploy all necessary resources to reduce crime, warning that, otherwise, major companies might be tempted to relocate elsewhere, such as London or Singapore. "Switzerland has a reputation for being a safe country where people feel secure, and it must stay that way," said Florence Schurch, secretary general of SuisseNégoce. "We are sounding the alarm because, unfortunately, things are changing."

The most urgent request concerns anonymizing real estate transactions in Geneva's official gazette, where they must be listed with the buyer's name and address. This information provides valuable information for "criminal gangs coming from France," according to SuisseNégoce. Unless they rely on clues gathered from social media, where the children of traders quickly display their lavish and unrestrained lifestyles.

In recent years, the official gazette has read like a health report for Geneva's flourishing trading industry, which handles a third of the world's oil. A few months ago, Richard Holtum, the head of the company Trafigura, bought a second villa, one kilometer from the first, in the affluent municipality of Collonge-Bellerive, at the edge of Lake Geneva. For 24.5 million Swiss francs (€26.6 million), the former British army captain can once again enjoy direct access to the lake. His residence, located in a 2,649-square-meter park, is shielded behind a two-meter hedge.

The purchase of a villa in 2022 in Pregny-Chambésy for 50 million Swiss francs by British national Mike Wainwright, the number two at Trafigura, had set the tone. Since then, employees of the oil trading giant have continued to snap up luxury properties: in recent years, these have included a 50 million Swiss franc mansion in Cologny, a 10 million villa in Vandœuvres and an exceptional 40 million residence in Collonge-Bellerive.

Big bonuses
The capital of this unlisted group is held by nearly 1,400 of its employees, who share the profits. However, just as in a football club, the star players – the traders who pull off the biggest deals – receive the largest bonuses. For the 2025 fiscal year, which ended on September 30, Trafigura paid approximately $3 billion (€2.56 billion) in dividends to its employees. That was down from the $6 billion peak in 2023, when the energy crisis after Russia's invasion of Ukraine pushed payouts sharply higher, but the company still said it remained highly profitable and resilient.

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz is not a catastrophe for everyone. Just like Trafigura, other industry leaders based in Geneva, such as Vitol, Mercuria and Gunvor (launched in 2000 by Gennady Timchenko, a close associate of Russian President Vladimir Putin, and Swedish billionaire Torbjorn Tornqvist), have benefited from the chaos unleashed on crude oil markets. The Financial Times reported on March 30 that the trading company Totsa, TotalEnergies' Geneva-based trading arm, scored a roughly $1 billion windfall just before the start of the US-Israeli strikes in Iran and the subsequent spike in oil prices, by locking in shipments of crude on supertankers.

Asked about these extraordinary windfall profits, none of the aforementioned Geneva-based companies responded to Le Monde's calls or messages. "These superprofits, starting with those of oil traders based in Switzerland, should be taxed immediately because they are the consequence of a conflict that will affect the cost of living here," argued Raphaël Mahaim, Green MP in the Swiss federal parliament in Bern. "These additional tax revenues could be used to fund public spending to mitigate the social effects of rising prices."

Co-author of a parliamentary proposal that stands no chance of being adopted (two-thirds of the Parliament leans to the right), Mahaim believes that in Switzerland, the lack of a true regulatory authority overseeing the commodities market – as the Swiss Financial Market Supervisory Authority does for the banking sector – "goes hand in hand with a deficit in regulation and transparency, which can facilitate questionable operations. There is a fairly widely shared view that this area of trading will be the next major international scandal to hit the country, just as banking secrecy did in the past."

Skilled and multilingual workforce
The non-governmental organization Public Eye, which has been tracking the rapid growth of these companies in Switzerland for about 15 years, has tried to measure their economic weight. This has not been easy, since none of the physical goods traded in the offices of traders in Geneva or Zug pass through Swiss territory, even though they travel to every corner of the globe. In the wake of the outbreak of the war in Ukraine, the volume of commodities trading orchestrated from Switzerland reached nearly 90 billion Swiss francs annually – close to 10% of the national gross domestic product.

"To illustrate how easily these companies operate here, it is worth noting that if they are found guilty of fraudulent practices, they are only given laughably small fines compared to their economic power: at most, five million Swiss francs. The penalties are much heavier in the United Kingdom or the United States," explained Adria Budry Carbo, an analyst at Public Eye.

While oil traders are concentrated in Geneva, Switzerland is home to 900 companies and nearly 10,000 jobs in commodities trading, from fossil fuels to metals and agricultural goods. To grasp the sector's power and diversity, it helps to understand that the price of Azeri Light crude from the Caspian Sea is set in Geneva, a bulk carrier of soybeans can be chartered between Santos in Brazil and Port Said in Egypt, and before sanctions against Russia, traders in Lugano were negotiating rolled metal sheets from the massive Magnitogorsk steel mill in the southern Urals.

Over the years, Switzerland has built up a comprehensive ecosystem, with a qualified, multilingual workforce that spans everything from trade finance and maritime shipping to law and tax firms and inspection companies. This chain of expertise has enabled Switzerland to reduce transaction costs while making complex deals easier to arrange. While the sector is mainly based in Geneva, it has also expanded along the Lake Geneva region, as well as into the small canton of Zug in central Switzerland, known for its favorable tax regime, and the canton of Ticino.

Increase transparency
Trading companies have chosen the country for a mix of tax advantages, political stability and a relatively flexible regulatory framework. "On the contrary, it is extremely rigid compared to other trading hubs such as Dubai and Singapore," countered Schurch of SuisseNégoce. "No one denies that there have been cases of corruption, but they took place more than 10 years ago. At that time, there was no legal framework regarding corruption, and since then, the profession has changed. As for regulating the sector further, we are already subject to all kinds of regulations, standards and sanctions from every country in which we purchase raw materials, as well as from all those in which we sell them, whether they are Swiss, European, American or international."

Since 2022, the Swiss Code of Obligations has required certain companies – particularly those operating in the commodities sector – to report on environmental, social, human rights and anti-corruption matters. These obligations also cover payments made to public bodies and issues such as child labor. A parliamentary initiative also aims to introduce due diligence and reporting requirements, as well as a market supervisory authority to enforce these obligations and increase transparency.

For trading companies, this kind of initiative is typical of the so-called "Swiss finish": an extra layer of national regulation that goes beyond international standards and could therefore put them at a disadvantage compared to competitors based in jurisdictions with even laxer rules. They point out that after Switzerland imposed sanctions on Russia in the spring of 2022, traders of Russian oil, starting with Rosneft Trading and Litasco, moved their operations to Dubai, from where they are believed to manage the Kremlin's infamous shadow fleet. Around 2010, Geneva handled as much as 80% of Russia's crude oil exports.

The Swiss paradox is clear: While the left would like to see tougher national legislation on these highly lucrative activities, no one at the local level seems bothered by the tax revenues they generate. The affluent village of Rolle, on the shores of Lake Geneva and just 30 minutes from Geneva itself, has seen a steady influx of often conspicuous expatriates, many of them from English-speaking countries. Take, for example, the drivers of two Lamborghini Revuelto cars – each worth €668,000 – who roar up the main street, their 1,015-horsepower V12 engines revving loudly. A local councilor commented, half-amused and half-exasperated: "We can't say we love these people, but even if they're undertaxed, it's their taxes that will pay for the new daycare center."
