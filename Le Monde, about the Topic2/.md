Compared to the UK, Switzerland remains a relatively safe haven for Russian oligarchs
As the two main hubs for billionaires close to the Kremlin like Viktor Rachnikov and Oleg Deripaska, London and Berne are not applying sanctions to Russia equally.
By Serge Enderlin (Geneva (Switzerland) correspondent)

Published on March 1, 2023, at 4:30 pm (Paris)

As was Stalin's intention in 1929, the gigantic steel mills in Magnitogorsk, Russia, are producing copious profits. They're almost entirely at the disposal of one man – Viktor Rachnikov, one of Russia's steel barons. He turned the plant from a cash-strapped mess into an export champion after the collapse of the Soviet Union.

But while the metal bars are emerging physically from a rolling mill at the foot of the Ural Mountains, they're being traded on the world market from the fourth floor of a glass building next to a Swiss lake. A piece of the Mediterranean at the end of the Alps, Lugano has attracted a small community of expatriates in a decade, to the point of becoming the "capital of Russian metal."

At MMK Trading, nobody answers the intercom. But on the telephone, a voice with a Russian accent abruptly hangs up after learning of Le Monde's interest in the firm's local activities. But this is definitely where Viktor Rashnikov set up his business – and his family – before the war. The man was worth €10 billion and owned three residences in Switzerland, along with a €300 million yacht with no less than six swimming pools on board. In the village of Porza, on the luxurious heights of Lugano, an official said that "the Rachnikov family has officially left the municipality." Similarly, the oligarchs Andrei Melnichenko and Alexei Mordachov – also heads of metallurgical conglomerates – have not been seen in the Ticino city since the beginning of the war.

One man knows a lot about them. He agreed to talk to Le Monde, but mostly only to note the damage that sanctions are inflicting on the fabric of the local economy. Alberto Genovesi is a renowned tax expert and director of Fidinam, an international consulting firm. The man behind the settlement of several large Russian industrial fortunes in the canton of Ticino, he sits on the board of directors of the Swiss Rashnikov companies and on the board of the Russian school in Lugano. "The sanctions against the Russians are a bad idea," he said. "They were supposed to stop the war, and we can see that it's having no effect. It's especially a big blow to Lugano. We mustn't forget that these companies hire staff, and pay salaries and taxes, something which keeps the local economy going. The loss of revenue easily reaches several tens of millions."

Cautious by vocation
Like other sanctioned individuals and companies, Viktor Rashnikov's MMK group had to stop all activity in the region. When contacted, the company's spokesperson in Moscow did not answer questions from Le Monde. It's rumored that international trading of the Magnitogorsk steel mills will now be conducted via Dubai or Singapore – as is the case for many Russian entities located elsewhere in Switzerland (Geneva and Zug), from where they used to sell crude oil and fossil or mineral raw materials.

However, despite the recent desertions, the general sense is that Switzerland is not in a hurry to complicate the lives of billionaires allied with Putin's regime. Cautious by vocation, the Swiss Bankers Association acknowledged that the level of Russian assets in the Confederation could reach 200 billion Swiss francs (€200 billion), a figure that may be significantly underestimated. However, the authorities have only "identified" 42 billion Swiss francs of this mountain of cash, and blocked only 7.5 billion.

Is this about not sending a negative signal to the Swiss financial center's very good Russian clients? "The extent of the Russian presence in Switzerland has never been completely mapped," explained Agathe Duparc, from the NGO Public Eye, which documents the infiltration of the Swiss economy by Russian capital. "The small amount of money blocked so far raises questions. We note that, unlike the United States, the United Kingdom and the European Union, Switzerland has still not created a task force to be able to move forward effectively."

Whether this is the usual Swiss slowness or something more deliberate, Berne rejects any notion of ill will. "We now have twenty people working on sanctions issues, and we're doing our utmost to find and freeze what needs to be frozen," said Erwin Bollinger, Director of the International Trade Department of the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO). "But be aware that many Russians have nothing to do with these sanctions, and we have no right to take any action." Swiss neutrality doesn't help with decision-making – it will be discussed in urgent debates in the Swiss parliament this Wednesday, March 1.

Political decision
The sanctions are indeed a political decision, taken against oligarchs accused of supporting – if not directly financing – a murderous regime. But on the Swiss side, there's a preference for sticking to a strict reading of property law. As long as wrongdoing can't be proven, it would be wrong to strike out in all directions.

An open letter from Geneva business lawyers, no doubt worried about losing some of their best clients in the long term, reads along the same lines. A few weeks ago, they denounced an "attack on the rights of individuals" in the sanctions that have hit the great fortunes of Russia in the West.

For once, the British are pulling no punches. In London, the most spectacular "seizure" has been the residence of the oligarch Oleg Deripaska. At 5 Belgrave Square – the most expensive square in the capital – the front door of his £50 million (€56.7 million) mansion has been walled up with plywood. In front, a homeless man placed his sleeping bag. "The government has finally reacted by going after the kleptocrats' billions," said Oliver Bullough, author of Butler to the World, on how the UK became the center of the offshore economy. "But this is still very inadequate. Barely 10% of what should have been tracked down has been tracked down."

In the City, American banker Bill Browder has been fighting the Putin regime for over a decade. He accuses senior Russian tax officials of stealing $230 million from him in 2011 when he was still an investment banker in the Russian capital. His Moscow lawyer, Sergei Magnitsky, died in prison under dubious conditions after exposing the fraud. Some of the money was found in Swiss accounts, but despite years of legal proceedings, a Swiss court has just dismissed the case and is going to send the money back... to Russia.

'A cultural attitude'
"The British were lax before the war. But since it started, they've been much more concerned, and more effective, than the Swiss," said Bill Browder. "There's something rotten in Switzerland. It's a cultural attitude. The Swiss like to be seen as 'neutral' so they can continue to handle dictators' money. It's also incompetence. They don't have the people to conduct high-level investigations but they could find them elsewhere with minimal motivation."

While the debate over the effectiveness of international sanctions against Putin's regime and its affiliates is not close to being settled, a new front is opening up. "The next step will be seizing funds. At the moment, they're just frozen," said Jamison Firestone, a London-based anti-corruption lawyer who was part of Bill Browder's original defense team. "Once seized, the idea would be to allocate them, for example, to rebuild Ukraine or to help people affected by Russia's illegal invasion." At present, the legal basis doesn't allow for such an intervention. It would in any case be very difficult to implement in London just as in the European Union.

Berne has already made it known that this potential step forward would be taken without it. "The guarantee of property is enshrined in the Constitution. There is no legal basis for the seizure of private Russian property," said a Federal Office of Justice working group last week. As the Swissinfo website recently pointed out, "The war in Ukraine has proven a delicate balancing act for Switzerland. On the one hand, it does not want to upset the US and Europe. On the other, it wants to uphold its reputation as a safe destination for the world's assets, beyond the reach of arbitrary measures."

Serge Enderlin (Geneva (Switzerland) correspondent)
